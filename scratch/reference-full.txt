                                    ABSTRACT

The increasing demand for electricity in urban areas has created a need for efficient energy monitoring, demand
forecasting, and environmental impact assessment. Variations in electricity consumption due to weather
conditions, seasonal changes, and time-based usage patterns can lead to periods of high demand and place
additional pressure on electricity infrastructure. At the same time, electricity generation contributes to carbon
emissions depending on the energy sources used. The Urban Energy Consumption, Emissions & Demand
Forecasting System aims to address these challenges through an integrated, data-driven approach to urban
energy analysis and planning.
The proposed system combines historical electricity consumption data, weather information, and time -based
features to identify consumption patterns, analyze peak-demand periods, and estimate associated carbon
emissions using suitable emission factors. Machine learning techniques, particularly the XGBoost regression
algorithm, are used to forecast electricity demand for the next 24 hours. The forecasting model is intended to be
evaluated using Mean Absolute Error, Root Mean Squared Error, and the coefficient of determination (R²). The
system also incorporates a web-based dashboard developed using React.js, supported by a FastAPI backend and
MongoDB database, to present consumption trends, emissions estimates, demand forecasts, and potenti al high-
demand alerts.
The project aims to make energy-related information easier to interpret and support informed decision-making
for urban energy management. By integrating data analytics, machine learning, and web technologies, the
system provides a foundation for understanding electricity usage and its environmental implications. It aligns
with Sustainable Development Goals 7, 11, and 13, relating to affordable and clean energy, sustainable cities
and communities, and climate action. The proposed system is intended to serve as an analytical and decision-
support platform that can be extended in the future through real-time data integration, renewable energy
analysis, and advanced energy optimization techniques.

                           CHAPTER 1 – INTRODUCTION

1.1 background

Electricity is one of the most essential resources for modern urban development. Residential buildings,
commercial establishments, industries, transportation systems, and public infrastructure depend on a continuous
and reliable supply of electrical energy. With the growth of urban populations and increasing dependence on
electrical appliances and digital infrastructure, the demand for electricity has become more dynamic and difficult
to manage.

Electricity consumption varies according to several factors, including the time of day, seasonal changes, weather
conditions, and the activities of consumers. For example, during periods of high temperature, the use of air
conditioners, coolers, and other cooling appliances may increase electricity demand significantly. Such
variations can create peak-demand periods and place additional pressure on electricity distribution
infrastructure.

Along with energy consumption, its environmental impact is another important consideration. The generation
of electricity may produce carbon emissions depending on the energy sources used, such as coal, natural gas,
solar, and wind. Therefore, understanding electricity consumption without examining its associated emissions
provides only a partial view of the overall energy situation.
Data analytics and machine learning provide opportunities to study historical consumption patterns, identify
periods of high demand, estimate electricity-related carbon emissions, and predict future electricity
requirements. By combining these capabilities within a single system, energy-related information can be
presented in a structured and understandable manner.

The Urban Energy Consumption, Emissions & Demand Forecasting System is proposed as a data -driven
platform that integrates electricity consumption analysis, carbon-emissions estimation, and electricity-demand
forecasting. It uses historical energy data, weather information, and time-based features to generate meaningful
insights. The system also incorporates a web-based dashboard to present analytical results, forecasts, and
potential high-demand alerts, supporting more informed and sustainable urban energy management.

1.2 Problem Statement

Urban electricity consumption fluctuates considerably due to variations in weather conditions, seasonal
requirements, time-based usage patterns, and consumer demand. During periods of increased electricity usage,
these fluctuations may place additional stress on energy infrastructure and make efficient energy management
more challenging.

                                                                         11
Electricity consumption is also associated with environmental impacts because the generation of electrical
energy may produce carbon emissions depending on the composition of the energy sources used. However,
consumption analysis, demand forecasting, and emissions estimation are often treated as separate activities.
This makes it difficult to obtain a unified understanding of energy usage, its environmental impact, and the
possibility of future demand increases.

The absence of an integrated analytical approach can limit the ability to identify high-consumption periods,
understand the relationship between electricity demand and weather conditions, and estimate the associated
carbon footprint. It also creates a need for better tools to anticipate future demand and identify situations in
which consumption may exceed predefined reference thresholds.

Therefore, the problem addressed by this project is the development of an integrated, data-driven system that
can analyze historical urban electricity consumption, estimate related carbon emissions, forecast electricity
demand for the next 24 hours, and generate alerts for potential high-demand situations. The system aims to
present these insights through an interactive web dashboard to support energy monitoring, planning, and
sustainable decision-making.

1.3 Motivation

The motivation behind this project is the growing need for efficient electricity management and environmental
sustainability in urban areas. Increasing energy requirements, changing weather conditions, and concerns
regarding carbon emissions make it important to understand not only how much electricity is consumed but also
when demand increases and what environmental impact is associated with that consumption.

One of the primary motivations is to identify electricity consumption patterns using historical data. Analyzing
hourly usage, seasonal variations, temperature, and previous demand values can help reveal the factors
associated with increased electricity requirements. Such insights can improve understanding of peak-demand
periods and support better planning.

Another motivation is the need to estimate the carbon emissions associated with electricity consumption. Since
electricity generation sources have different emission characteristics, combining consumption data with suitable
emission factors can provide a clearer picture of the environmental impact of energy usage.

The project also provides an opportunity to apply concepts from computer science and technology to a practical
infrastructure-related problem. It combines data preprocessing, exploratory data analysis, machine learning,
database management, backend development, frontend development, and data visualization within a single
application.

The use of the XGBoost regression algorithm for electricity-demand forecasting is intended to identify
relationships between historical demand, weather conditions, and time-based features. Furthermore, the

                                                                         12
integration of React.js, FastAPI, and MongoDB provides a framework for presenting the results through a
unified web application.
Thus, the project is motivated by the opportunity to combine machine learning, full -stack development, and
sustainability-oriented data analytics to address an important urban energy management challenge.

1.4 Objectives of the Project

The main objective of the Urban Energy Consumption, Emissions & Demand Forecasting System is to develop
an integrated analytical platform for understanding urban electricity consumption, estimating associated carbon
emissions, and forecasting future electricity demand.
The specific objectives are as follows:
1. To analyze electricity consumption patterns: Study historical electricity usage to identify daily trends,
seasonal variations, peak-demand periods, and changes in consumption over time.
2. To estimate carbon emissions: Calculate electricity-related carbon emissions using appropriate emission
factors and consumption values to understand the environmental impact of energy usage.
3. To forecast electricity demand: Develop a machine learning-based forecasting model using the XGBoost
regression algorithm to predict electricity demand for the next 24 hours using historical consumption, weather,
and time-based features.
4. To develop an interactive dashboard: Build a web-based interface that presents energy consumption trends,
emissions estimates, demand forecasts, and other relevant insights through charts and visualizations.
5. To identify potential high-demand situations: Compare forecasted demand with predefined reference or
threshold values and generate suitable alerts when demand is expected to exceed those values.
6. To integrate the system components: Combine data processing, machine learning, database operations,
backend services, and frontend visualization into a unified application.
7. To support sustainable energy management: Provide data-driven insights that can help users understand
energy usage, assess environmental impact, and make more informed decisions regarding urban electricity
planning.

1.5 Scope of the Project

The scope of the project is to develop a data-driven software system for analyzing urban electricity consumption,
estimating related carbon emissions, and forecasting future electricity demand. The system focuses on the use

                                                                         13
of historical electricity data, weather information, and time-based features to study consumption patterns and
generate useful analytical insights.
The first major component focuses on electricity consumption analysis. It involves collecting and preprocessing
suitable datasets, handling missing or inconsistent values, and analyzing variations in demand across different
time periods. The analysis is intended to identify peak-demand periods, daily and seasonal trends, and possible
relationships between electricity usage and environmental conditions.
The second component focuses on carbon-emissions estimation. It uses electricity consumption values and
suitable emission factors to estimate the emissions associated with energy usage. The resulting estimates can be
presented through visualizations to help users understand the environmental implications of electricity
consumption.
The third component involves machine learning-based demand forecasting. The project uses XGBoost

forecasted demand with predefined thresholds. These alerts are intended to provide early indications of possible
demand-related concerns.
The project is limited to data analysis, demand forecasting, visualization, and decision support using the
available datasets. Direct control of physical electricity infrastructure, guaranteed prediction of blackouts, and
real-time intervention in the electricity grid are outside its scope. The reliability of the analytical results depends
on the quality, completeness, and suitability of the available data.

2.1 Introduction to Urban Energy Management

Urban energy management has become an important area of research due to increasing electricity consumption,
changing weather conditions, and the need to improve energy efficiency in cities. Residential buildings,
commercial establishments, industries, and public infrastructure contribute to electricity demand, which varies
according to time, season, temperature, and consumer activities. Understanding these variations is essential for
identifying peak-demand periods and supporting effective energy planning.
Traditional energy monitoring approaches primarily focus on recording and displaying electricity consumption.
However, consumption data alone may not provide sufficient information about future demand or the
environmental impact associated with electricity generation. An integrated approach that combines energy
analytics, carbon-emissions estimation, and demand forecasting can provide a more comprehensive
understanding of urban electricity usage.
Recent developments in data analytics and machine learning have created opportunities to process historical
energy data, identify consumption patterns, and predict future electricity requirements. Weather information and
time-based variables can be incorporated into forecasting models to improve their ability to represent changes
in electricity demand. Similarly, emission factors can be used to estimate the carbon emissions associated with
electricity consumption.
The Urban Energy Consumption, Emissions & Demand Forecasting System follows this integrated approach
by combining electricity consumption analysis, emissions estimation, and machine learning-based demand
forecasting within a web-based application. This chapter reviews the major approaches related to these
components and identifies the research gap addressed by the proposed system.

2.2 Existing Energy Monitoring Systems

Energy monitoring systems are designed to collect, process, and present information about electricity usage.
Such systems may use smart meters, historical consumption records, or other energy-monitoring data sources
to provide insights into consumption patterns.
Basic monitoring systems generally present electricity usage through numerical summaries, tables, and
graphical representations. These features help users understand consumption levels over different periods and
identify intervals in which electricity usage is relatively high. More advanced systems may provide time-based
comparisons, consumption breakdowns, and visualizations to support energy-management decisions.

                                                                         17
Smart-meter-based monitoring provides more detailed consumption information than occasional manual
readings. Timestamped readings can be used to study hourly, daily, and seasonal changes in electricity usage.
When such information is organized by geographical zones or consumer categories, it can also support
comparisons between different parts of an urban area.
However, monitoring historical consumption does not automatically provide a reliable forecast of future
demand. Similarly, a system that only displays electricity usage may not estimate the associated carbon
emissions. These limitations create an opportunity to combine historical monitoring with predictive analytics
and environmental assessment.
The proposed project extends the concept of energy monitoring by bringing consumption analysis, emissions
estimation, and electricity-demand forecasting together in a unified dashboard.

2.3 Electricity Demand Forecasting Techniques

Electricity demand forecasting involves estimating future electricity requirements using historical demand
observations and relevant influencing factors. Forecasting can support energy planning by providing an estimate
of expected consumption and helping identify periods when demand may become unusually high.
Several approaches can be used for electricity-demand forecasting, depending on the available data, prediction
horizon, and complexity of consumption patterns.
2.3.1 Statistical Forecasting Methods
Statistical methods use historical observations to estimate future values. Time -series techniques examine
patterns such as trends, seasonality, and relationships between observations at different time intervals.
These methods can provide useful forecasts when historical data exhibits relatively stable and identifiable
patterns. However, their performance may be affected when demand depends on complex interactions between
weather, time, and other variables.
2.3.2 Traditional Machine Learning Methods
Traditional machine learning algorithms learn relationships between input variables and a target value from
historical data. For electricity forecasting, input variables may include previous demand, hour of the day, day
of the week, month, and temperature.
Regression-based methods can model relationships between these features and electricity demand. Their
effectiveness depends on the characteristics of the dataset, the selection of input features, and the way the model
is trained and evaluated.

                                                                         18
2.3.3 XGBoost Regression
XGBoost is a machine learning algorithm based on gradient-boosted decision trees. It builds an ensemble of
trees sequentially, with later trees helping reduce errors made by the existing ensemble.
XGBoost can model nonlinear relationships and interactions between different input variables. This makes it a
suitable candidate for forecasting tasks in which electricity demand may depend on combinations of historical
usage, weather conditions, and time-based characteristics.

                                             Figure 2.1 Load Forecasting process
In the proposed system, XGBoost regression is selected for electricity-demand forecasting. Features such as
historical demand values, hour, day, month, and available weather variables can be used as model inputs. The
intended prediction horizon is the next 24 hours.

The model's performance should be evaluated using appropriate metrics, including Mean Absolute Error
(MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R²). The actual suitability of
the model must be established from the results obtained during implementation and testing.

2.4 Carbon Emissions Analysis Approaches

Carbon-emissions analysis examines the environmental impact associated with energy consumption.
Electricity-related emissions depend on the amount of electricity consumed and the emission characteristics of
the sources used to generate it.
A commonly used approach is to estimate emissions by multiplying electricity consumption by an appropriate
emission factor. The emission factor represents the estimated emissions associated with a unit of electricity and
must be selected according to the relevant dataset, geographical context, and calculation methodology.

                                                                         19
The general calculation is:
                            CO2e Emissions = Electricity Consumption × Emission Factor

For example, when electricity consumption is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh) and the emission factor is
expressed in kilograms of CO₂-equivalent per kWh, the resulting estimate is expressed in kilograms of CO₂-
equivalent.
The accuracy of this estimate depends on the suitability of the emission factor and the quality of the consumption
data. Where a fixed emission factor is used, the resulting emissions estimate will reflect that factor's
assumptions. More detailed approaches may account for changes in the electricity generation mix over time.
The project incorporates emissions estimation alongside consumption analysis so that users can examine energy
usage and its estimated environmental impact within the same system. The exact emission factors and their
sources should be documented in the implementation chapter according to those actually used in the project.

2.5 Review of Related Research

Several research studies have explored electricity-demand forecasting, smart-meter-based energy analysis, and
the application of machine learning in power systems. These studies provide a foundation for selecting
appropriate forecasting techniques and identifying useful input features for the proposed project.
2.5.1 Load Forecasting Using Smart Meter Data
Semmelmann, Henni, and Weinhardt (2022), in their research paper “Load forecasting for energy communities:
a novel LSTM-XGBoost hybrid model based on smart meter data,” investigated day-ahead electricity-load
forecasting using smart-meter data. The researchers proposed a hybrid model combining a bidirectional Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network with XGBoost to forecast general consumption patterns and peak loads.
The study found that smart-meter information could improve forecasting accuracy compared with approaches
based on standard load profiles. It also highlighted the importance of historical consumption and time -related
features in predicting electricity demand. This research supports the use of historical energy data and machine
learning for short-term demand forecasting.

2.5.2 Short-Term Load Forecasting Under Extreme Weather Conditions
A 2022 study published in Energy Reports, titled “Bagging–XGBoost algorithm based extreme weather
identification and short-term load forecasting model,” examined electricity-demand forecasting during extreme
weather conditions.

                                                                         20
The researchers combined XGBoost with a bagging approach and considered weather-related variables,
historical load, and time-based features. The study focused on identifying periods of extreme demand and
improving short-term load forecasting. Its findings demonstrate the relevance of incorporating weather
conditions into forecasting systems, particularly when unusual weather may influence electricity consumption.

ScienceDirect

This study is relevant to the proposed project because weather variables such as temperature can influence
electricity demand, especially when cooling requirements increase during hot periods.

2.5.3 Data-Driven Carbon Emissions Estimation in Urban Areas

Research on urban carbon-emissions estimation has explored the relationship between energy consumption data
and standard emission factors. A 2025 study published in Sustainability, titled “From Energy Dependence to
Spatial Intelligence: A Spatial Data-Based Carbon Emission Estimation Model for Urban Built-Up Area,”
examined the estimation of carbon emissions across urban areas using energy-related data and emission factors.

The study considered different urban sectors and demonstrated how energy-consumption information can be
incorporated into emissions-estimation calculations. This approach is relevant to the proposed project because
it establishes the importance of connecting energy usage with its estimated environmental impact.

consumption is combined with an appropriate emission factor to estimate associated carbon emissions.

2.6 Research Gap

The literature review indicates that electricity-demand forecasting and urban carbon-emissions estimation are
important areas of energy research. Existing studies demonstrate the usefulness of machine learning algorithms
for predicting electricity demand and the use of emission factors for estimating the environmental impact of
energy consumption.

However, the requirements of an integrated energy-analysis platform extend beyond individual forecasting or
emissions calculations. Consumption patterns, environmental impact, future electricity requirements, and the
presentation of results need to be considered together to provide a useful overview of urban energy usage.

The following research gaps motivate the proposed project:

1. Integration of analytical components: Energy-consumption analysis, carbon-emissions estimation, and
demand forecasting may be implemented as separate functions. The proposed project aims to bring these
components together within a single application.

2. Incorporation of multiple influencing factors: Electricity demand is affected by historical usage, time -based
patterns, and weather conditions. The project aims to combine available features to develop a more informative
demand-forecasting model.

3. Environmental impact assessment: Consumption monitoring alone does not indicate the estimated carbon
emissions associated with electricity usage. The project incorporates emissions estimation alongside
consumption analysis.

4. Accessible presentation of results: Analytical findings are more useful when they can be interpreted easily.
The proposed system uses an interactive web dashboard to present consumption trends, emissions estimates,
demand forecasts, and potential high-demand alerts.

5. Decision-support functionality: Forecasting future demand and comparing it with predefined thresholds can
help identify potential high-demand situations before they occur. The project aims to provide such alerts as an
analytical aid rather than directly controlling physical grid infrastructure.

Based on these gaps, the Urban Energy Consumption, Emissions & Demand Forecasting System proposes an
integrated approach combining data preprocessing, electricity-consumption analysis, carbon-emissions
estimation, XGBoost-based demand forecasting, and web-based visualization. The system is intended to support
better understanding of energy usage and provide information that may assist sustainable urban energy planning.

The project does not claim to introduce a new machine learning algorithm. Its primary contribution is the
integration of established data-analysis and forecasting techniques into a unified application focused on
electricity consumption, environmental impact, and future demand.

3.1 Proposed Methodology

The Urban Energy Consumption, Emissions & Demand Forecasting System, named VoltAtlas — Urban Energy
Grid & Emissions Analyzer, follows a data-driven methodology that combines energy consumption analysis,
carbon-emissions estimation, machine learning-based demand forecasting, and interactive data visualization.
The system is developed as a simulated Delhi NCR case study to demonstrate how energy analytics and
forecasting can be applied to an urban environment. It does not connect to a live electricity grid or use actual
smart-meter readings. Instead, it generates a reproducible dataset containing 120 consecutive days of hourly
observations across six simulated urban districts.
The methodology consists of data generation, preprocessing, database storage, consumption analysis, emissions
estimation, feature engineering, model training, forecasting, alert generation, and dashboard integration.
FastAPI provides the backend services, SQLite stores the generated records and related data, and XGBoost is
used for electricity-demand forecasting. The frontend is developed using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript.
The major stages of the proposed methodology are described below.
1. Data Generation: Generate hourly electricity demand and weather-related observations for six simulated
urban districts using a fixed random seed to support reproducibility.

4.7 Demand Forecasting Using XGBoost

4.7.1 Overview
The Demand Forecast module uses XGBoost regression to estimate electricity demand for the next 24 hours.
XGBoost is a gradient-boosting algorithm that combines multiple decision trees to learn relationships between
input features and a target variable.
For VoltAtlas, the target variable is electricity demand. The input variables include calendar features, weather
measurements, historical demand lags, and rolling statistics.
The forecasting pipeline includes the following stages:

    1. Prepare the historical hourly observations.
    2. Generate the calendar, weather, lag, and rolling features.
    3. Divide the observations chronologically into training and testing sets.
    4. Train the XGBoost regression model.
    5. Evaluate the model on held-out observations.
    6. Compare its performance with a lag-24 persistence baseline.
    7. Generate the next 24 hourly predictions recursively.
    8. Return the forecast to the dashboard.
4.7.2 Chronological Training and Testing
The project uses a chronological 80:20 split. Earlier observations are used for training, while later observations
are reserved for testing. This approach is more appropriate for time-series forecasting than randomly mixing
observations from different times.

4.7.5 Recursive 24-Hour Forecasting

After training, the application generates the next 24 hourly demand predictions. The process is recursive because
each newly predicted demand value may be used as an input when preparing later forecast steps.

At each step, the model uses the available calendar and weather inputs together with the relevant lagged and
rolling demand features. Once a prediction is produced, the prediction sequence is updated before the next step.

This approach allows the application to generate a continuous 24-hour forecast. However, prediction errors may
accumulate over successive steps. The held-out one-step-ahead metrics should therefore not be presented as the
verified error of the complete 24-hour recursive forecast.

The forecast also uses the previous day's hourly weather pattern as a scenario assumption instead of obtaining
future weather predictions from an external weather service.

5.5 Model Evaluation Using MAE, RMSE and R²

The forecasting model is evaluated using three standard regression metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root
Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R²). The project also includes a lag -24
persistence baseline for comparison.
5.5.1 Mean Absolute Error
MAE calculates the average absolute difference between actual and predicted demand values.
MAE = (1/n) Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|
A lower MAE indicates that the predictions are, on average, closer to the observed values. Since demand is
measured in MW, the MAE is also expressed in MW.
5.5.2 Root Mean Squared Error
RMSE is the square root of the average squared prediction errors.
RMSE = √[(1/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²]
RMSE gives greater weight to larger prediction errors. A lower RMSE generally indicates better predictive
performance on the evaluated observations.
5.5.3 Coefficient of Determination
R² measures how well the model's predictions explain the variation in the observed target values. A value closer
to 1 generally indicates a better fit on the evaluated dataset. Its interpretation depends on the data and evaluation
setting, and it should not be used alone to establish model quality.
5.5.4 Comparison with the Persistence Baseline
The project compares XGBoost predictions with a lag-24 persistence baseline. This baseline uses demand from
24 hours earlier as a simple reference prediction.

                                                                         57
The metrics recorded during testing are one-step-ahead evaluation results using observed historical lag values.
They should not be presented as the measured error of the complete 24-hour recursive forecast. Evaluating the
complete forecast horizon would require a separate walk-forward evaluation procedure.
Until the metric values are inserted and compared, no conclusion should be made that XGBoost outperforms
the persistence baseline.

6.3 Limitations

Despite its useful features, the current implementation has certain limitations that should be considered when
interpreting its outputs.
1. Use of synthetic data: The application generates 120 days of hourly observations for six simulated urban
districts. The patterns may not fully represent the behaviour of an actual electricity distribution network.
2. Absence of live data integration: The current version does not directly collect readings from smart meters,
electricity utilities, or live weather services. Consequently, the dashboard reflects the generated dataset rather
than current grid conditions.
3. Illustrative emissions factors: The emission factors used for coal, gas, solar, and wind are illustrative
assumptions. They are not presented as verified, current Delhi NCR grid-emission values. The estimated
emissions therefore depend on the assumptions configured in the application.
4. Forecasting limitations: The model's evaluation uses a chronological training and testing split. The reported
offline metrics are based on one-step-ahead predictions using observed lagged demand. The displayed 24-hour

                                                                         64
forecast is recursive, meaning that earlier predicted values are reused to generate later predictions. Its accuracy
may differ from the offline test metrics.
5. District-level forecast allocation: Forecasts for individual districts are derived using fixed proportional
demand shares rather than separate forecasting models trained for every district. This may not capture district-
specific consumption patterns.
6. Weather assumptions: For the future 24-hour forecast, the application reuses the previous day's hourly weather
pattern as a scenario assumption. This is not equivalent to using an actual weather forecast.
7. Simplified alerts and health indicators: Capacity alerts identify demand exceeding a configured threshold;
they do not calculate the probability of a blackout. Similarly, the district health score is a heuristic indicator
based on selected factors and should not be interpreted as a certified assessment of grid safety.
8. Prototype-level deployment: The current application is intended for local academic use. Further work would
be required to establish production-grade authentication, security, data governance, reliability, and integration
with operational infrastructure.
These limitations provide clear directions for improving the application's reliability and extending its practical
usefulness.

A future version could integrate verified smart-meter readings, utility consumption records, and weather APIs.
Automated data ingestion and validation would help the system analyse actual electricity consumption instead
of relying on synthetic observations.
6.4.2 Improved Demand Forecasting
The forecasting module could be trained and tested using larger, verified datasets covering different seasons and
demand conditions. Additional approaches, such as Random Forest, LightGBM, LSTM networks, and other
time-series models, could be compared with XGBoost. Walk-forward evaluation and direct multi-step
forecasting could also be explored to assess the accuracy of the complete 24-hour forecast more realistically.
6.4.3 More Reliable Carbon Emissions Estimation

                                                                         65
Illustrative emission factors could be replaced with verified, regionally appropriate data. Incorporating time -
varying generation-mix information would allow emissions estimates to reflect changes in the sources supplying
electricity. The system could also display the assumptions and uncertainty associated with each estimate.
6.4.4 District-Specific Forecasting
Instead of allocating overall demand forecasts through fixed proportions, separate models could be trained for
individual districts if sufficient district-level historical data becomes available. This could help capture local
demand patterns, weather sensitivity, and differences in consumption behaviour.
6.4.5 Advanced Alerts and Anomaly Detection
Future development could include anomaly detection to identify unusual consumption patterns and improved
alerting based on verified capacity information. With suitable operational data, the system could support more
informative warnings and help users investigate possible causes of unusual demand.
6.4.6 Enhanced Dashboard and Deployment
The dashboard could be extended with user accounts, role-based access, automated report generation, scheduled
updates, and secure cloud deployment. Additional maps and comparison tools could help users examine
geographical differences in energy consumption and emissions.
6.4.7 Support for Energy Efficiency Planning
With reliable data, the application could help identify periods of high demand, compare energy-use patterns,
and examine the potential effect of energy-efficiency measures. These insights could support preliminary
planning by researchers, students, and energy analysts.
The above improvements would move VoltAtlas from a reproducible academic prototype towards a more data-
driven energy analysis platform. Their implementation would depend on the availability of reliable data, suitable
validation procedures, and the requirements of the intended users.

6.5 Contribution Towards Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

VoltAtlas is aligned with selected United Nations Sustainable Development Goals through its focus on energy
analysis, urban sustainability, and emissions awareness.
SDG 7: Affordable and Clean Energy
The project supports awareness of electricity consumption and estimated emissions associated with different
energy sources. Its analytics and forecasting features demonstrate how data-driven tools can contribute to
energy-efficiency planning and better understanding of demand patterns.
SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities

                                                                         66
By presenting simulated district-level consumption, forecasts, and energy indicators, VoltAtlas illustrates how
digital tools can support the study of urban energy requirements. The approach could be extended to help urban
planners and researchers examine energy-related challenges using verified local data.
SDG 13: Climate Action
The emissions estimation module connects electricity consumption with estimated carbon dioxide equivalent
emissions. By making these estimates visible alongside demand patterns, the project encourages consideration
of the environmental impact of energy use.
The project's contribution to these goals is primarily demonstrative and educational. Since the present version
uses synthetic data and illustrative emission factors, it does not establish measurable reductions in energy
consumption or greenhouse gas emissions. Such outcomes would require deployment with reliable real-world
data and evaluation of the effect of decisions informed by the system.

              APPENDIX B: API ENDPOINTS AND EXECUTION

VoltAtlas provides REST API endpoints through FastAPI to retrieve energy data, access forecasts, manage
settings, and export information. The available API documentation can be accessed locally after starting the
application.
Important endpoints:

    • GET /api/health – Checks application health.
    • GET /api/overview – Retrieves overview information.
    • GET /api/timeseries – Retrieves historical energy data.
    • GET /api/zones – Retrieves district-level information.
    • GET /api/forecast – Retrieves the electricity demand forecast.
    • GET /api/alerts – Retrieves generated alerts.
    • GET /api/insights – Retrieves analytical insights.
    • GET /api/export – Exports supported data in CSV format.
    • POST /api/retrain – Initiates model retraining.
Application execution:
The application can be started from the project directory using:
python start.py
After the server starts, the dashboard is accessible at http://localhost:8000. The interactive API documentation
is available at http://localhost:8000/docs.
Automated tests can be executed using:
python -m pytest -q
These commands provide a convenient way to launch the application and check its implemented functionality
in the local development environment.
